How the Part B late enrollment penalty is calculated

By Which Senior Plan. Last checked 20 Nov 2026.

General information from public pages. This is not insurance advice, not a recommendation, and not a government site.

A penalty is not IRMAA. The 10 percent clock below is for signing up late. Income can add a separate amount. The 2027 IRMAA brackets start above $112,000 for a single filer. The premium those percentages sit on is the 2027 Part B premium of $209.40.

Short version: Medicare.gov adds 10 percent of the standard Part B premium for each full 12 months you could have had Part B and did not. The extra is part of the monthly premium for as long as you have Part B. It is not a one time fee. A partial year does not count. If you missed the first window and you do not have a special enrollment period, you can sign up from January 1 through March 31. Coverage starts the month after you sign up.

The 10 percent rule

Count full 12 month periods only. One full year without Part B is 10 percent. Two full years is 20 percent. Three is 30 percent. Medicare.gov then adds that percentage of the standard premium to the standard premium, and rounds the sum to the nearest 10 cents. The standard premium changes every year, so the dollar penalty changes even when the percentage stays put. Higher income can raise the bill again, on top of this math. That income surcharge is IRMAA, and it uses a tax return, not the number of months you waited.

You generally do not owe the Part B penalty if you qualify for a special enrollment period, or if you are in a Medicare Savings Program. Job coverage has to be from current work. COBRA does not keep the Part B special period open. The limits are on the special enrollment page.

Medicare.gov's own examples

These two blocks are Medicare.gov's examples, copied as arithmetic from the avoid penalties page read 20 Nov 2026. They are not a bill for a named person.

For 2026, a wait of 2 full years (24 months) is a 20 percent penalty. Twenty percent of the $202.90 standard premium is $40.58. Added to $202.90 that is $243.48, which Medicare.gov rounds to $243.50 for 2026.

For 2027, the same 2 year wait is still 20 percent, now of the $209.40 standard premium. Twenty percent is $41.88. Added to $209.40 that is $251.28, which Medicare.gov rounds to $251.30 for 2027.

A hypothetical with a different delay

Hypothetical, not a Medicare.gov example: someone waits 3 full 12 month periods and does not have a special enrollment period. The percentage is 30. Thirty percent of the 2027 standard premium is 0.30 times $209.40, which is $62.82. Added to $209.40 that is $272.22. Using the same nearest 10 cents rounding Medicare.gov shows in its examples, $272.22 becomes $272.20 a month in 2027. A wait of 30 months would still be only 2 full 12 month periods. The extra 6 months would not add another 10 percent. Do not treat this paragraph as your premium. Social Security calculates the real one.

Part A uses a different clock

Only people who pay a premium for Part A can owe a Part A penalty. Medicare.gov says the monthly premium may go up 10 percent, and you pay the higher premium for twice the number of years you could have signed up but did not. The example on that page: eligible for 2 years and not signed up, the higher premium lasts 4 years. It then stops. That is the part that does not match Part B.

CMS, on 17 Nov 2026, set the 2027 Part A premiums at $328 a month with at least 30 quarters of work, and $597 with fewer than 30. The 2026 bases were $311 and $565. Hypothetical, using only those published premiums and Medicare.gov's 10 percent rule: 10 percent of $328 is $32.80, so the higher 2027 premium would be $360.80. Ten percent of $597 is $59.70, so $656.70. If the uncovered stretch was 2 years, those higher amounts would last 4 years. CMS did not publish those penalty totals. They are the arithmetic, labeled so they are not mistaken for a fact sheet line.

Part D is 1 percent a month

The Part D penalty is 1 percent of the national base beneficiary premium for each full month you went without Part D or other creditable drug coverage after you were first eligible. Medicare.gov uses a gap of 63 days in a row, after the Initial Enrollment Period, as the point where the penalty can start. The monthly penalty is rounded to the nearest 10 cents, added to the plan premium, and it lasts as long as you have drug coverage, even if you change plans. The base changes, so the dollar amount can change each year. The base is not the premium printed on your plan.

Medicare.gov's example is a 14 month gap. The national base is $38.99 in 2026 and $41.33 in 2027. Fourteen percent of $38.99 is $5.46, rounded to $5.50 in 2026. Fourteen percent of $41.33 is $5.79, rounded to $5.80 in 2027. Both figures are added to the plan premium for that year.

Hypothetical, for a 20 month gap in 2027: 20 percent of $41.33 is $8.266, which rounds to $8.30 a month under the same nearest 10 cents step. That illustration is not on the Medicare.gov example. You generally avoid the Part D penalty if you had creditable drug coverage, or if you get Extra Help. Extra Help also stops the penalty while you have it. Drug plan rules for 2026, including the $2,100 cap, are on the Part D page. The 2027 cap of $2,400 is on the 2027 costs page.

Questions people ask

How is the Part B late enrollment penalty calculated?

Medicare.gov adds 10 percent for each full 12 months you could have had Part B but did not. A 2 year wait is 20 percent of that year's standard premium, added to the premium and rounded to the nearest 10 cents. In the 2027 example that is $251.30 a month.

Does the Part B penalty ever stop?

Medicare.gov says you pay it for as long as you have Part B. It is not a one time late fee. The Part A penalty is the one that runs for twice the number of years you delayed and then ends.

Is the Part D penalty the same dollar amount every year?

No. It is recalculated from that year's national base beneficiary premium. Medicare.gov lists $38.99 for 2026 and $41.33 for 2027. Its 14 month example is $5.50 in 2026 and $5.80 in 2027.
