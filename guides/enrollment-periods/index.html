Medicare enrollment periods and deadlines

By Which Senior Plan. Last checked 20 Nov 2026.

General information from public pages. This is not insurance advice, not a recommendation, and not a government site.

Missed Part B? That is a different clock from plan switching. Read how the Part B late penalty is calculated, or stay here if you are only changing an Advantage or drug plan during October 15 to December 7.

Short version: Your first signup window is 7 months around your 65th birthday. Each fall, October 15 through December 7, you can change Advantage and drug plans for January 1. January 1 through March 31 is both the general enrollment period for Part A and Part B and a separate window if you are already in Medicare Advantage. Those two January windows do not do the same job.

These windows are not interchangeable. Missing the fall deadline does not open the Medigap window, and the January Advantage window does not let someone with only Original Medicare join an Advantage plan. Each page below stays inside one set of rules.

Signing up for Part B is not the same as joining a plan

Medicare.gov's joining a plan page, read 20 Nov 2026, keeps the two steps apart. You need both Part A and Part B to join a Medicare Advantage plan. You need either Part A or Part B to join a standalone drug plan. You also have to live in the plan's service area. If you live in another state for part of the year, Medicare.gov says to check whether that plan will cover you there before you join.

When the plan starts depends on which window you used. During fall open enrollment, coverage starts January 1, and the plan has to have the request by December 7. During Medicare Advantage open enrollment, coverage starts the first day of the month after the plan gets the request. That January window is not only January 1 through March 31. Medicare.gov also lists the first 3 months you have Medicare, and only if you are already in an Advantage plan. Someone who joined an Advantage plan during the Initial Enrollment Period can change to another Advantage plan, or go back to Original Medicare with or without a drug plan, within the first 3 months they have both Part A and Part B.

There is a second Initial Enrollment Period for people who already have Part A and add Part B later. Medicare.gov says it starts 3 months before the month you are first eligible for both, and you have 2 months after you have both for the first time. Plan coverage in that window generally starts the first of the month after the plan gets the request. If you sign up for Part A or Part B during a special enrollment period because of an exceptional situation, the same page gives you 2 months to join a plan. The situations themselves are on the special enrollment page, not a single calendar date.

The General Enrollment Period is the Part A and Part B door if you missed the first window and you do not have a special period. It runs January 1 through March 31, and coverage starts the month after you sign up. A penalty can follow. The arithmetic is on the penalty page, including the 2027 example Medicare.gov now prints. Medigap is still its own 6 month clock, and several states rewrite it. New York keeps applications open all year. California adds a birthday window. Texas keeps the one time six months and splits the under 65 rules.

Questions people ask

Is October 15 the only time I can change plans?

No. It is the main yearly window for Advantage and drug plans. Other windows apply when you first join Medicare, when you already have an Advantage plan in the first quarter or in your first 3 months with Medicare, and when you have a special situation.

Does the fall window reset my Medigap protections?

No. Medigap open enrollment is a one time 6 month period in most states. Medicare.gov says it does not repeat every year the way Medicare open enrollment does. New York is one state that keeps Medigap applications open all year.

What if I miss a deadline?

You may have to wait for the next general or open enrollment period, and you may owe a late enrollment penalty. The penalty page shows Medicare.gov's 10 percent rule and the 2026 and 2027 examples.
